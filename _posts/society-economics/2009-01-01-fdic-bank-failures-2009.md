## Summary

The United States banking sector experienced a wave of failures in 2009, with 140 FDIC-insured commercial banks and savings institutions closing — the highest annual total since 1992, when 181 institutions failed during the closing years of the Savings & Loan (S&L) crisis. These institutions collectively held roughly $170 billion in total assets. The cascade of failures represented a dramatic acceleration from 25 bank failures in 2008, itself already elevated well above the near-zero failure rates of the mid-2000s. The count would rise further to 157 failures in 2010, the peak of the crisis-era wave.

Colonial Bank's collapse was the largest bank failure of 2009 and, at the time, the sixth-largest in U.S. history by assets. Separately, IndyMac Federal Bank — the FDIC-managed successor to IndyMac Bancorp, which had failed in July 2008 — was sold to a consortium of private investors forming OneWest Bank in March 2009, one of the largest resolutions of a failed thrift since the S&L crisis.

## Root causes

**Residential real estate exposure**: Most 2009 failures were community and regional banks concentrated in construction lending, land development, and residential mortgage portfolios. When home prices fell 30–40% in key markets (Florida, California, Nevada, Arizona), these loan books became severely impaired.

**Commercial real estate (CRE) deterioration**: As the recession deepened through 2009, vacancy rates in retail, office, and industrial properties rose sharply, pushing many CRE loans into non-performance at banks that had diversified away from residential but into CRE instead.

**Capital depletion**: Banks that had paid dividends, expanded aggressively, or relied on brokered deposits found their regulatory capital ratios dropping below "well-capitalized" thresholds (10% Tier 1 capital ratio), triggering regulatory corrective action and eventual closure.

**Credit crunch feedback loop**: As regional banks failed or contracted lending, businesses and consumers lost credit access, deepening the recession, which in turn increased bank loan losses — a self-reinforcing cycle.

## FDIC response and deposit insurance

The FDIC's Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) was stressed by the volume of payouts. Its reserve ratio fell to 0.22% of insured deposits by mid-2009 — far below the statutory 1.15% minimum — and the fund balance turned negative in the third quarter of 2009. In response, the FDIC imposed a special emergency assessment on insured institutions in mid-2009 and, in December 2009, required banks to pre-pay roughly three years of assessments to replenish reserves without drawing on its Treasury credit line.

The temporary increase in the deposit insurance ceiling from $100,000 to $250,000 per depositor per institution, enacted by the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of October 2008, was effective through December 2013 (later made permanent) and prevented widespread depositor panic runs analogous to those of the 1930s.

## Trajectory and aftermath

Bank failures continued to rise after 2009, peaking at 157 in 2010, before declining year by year: 92 in 2011, 51 in 2012, and into the single digits by 2014. By the time the crisis-era failure wave ended, more than 500 FDIC-insured institutions had failed between 2008 and 2015, with total losses to the DIF exceeding $90 billion. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (July 2010) subsequently strengthened resolution authority and raised the minimum DIF ratio target to 1.35%.
