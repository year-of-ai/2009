# Tropical Storm Aila 2009

**Date:** May 25–26, 2009  
**Location:** Bangladesh (Khulna, Satkhira, Bagerhat districts); India (West Bengal — 24 Parganas, Howrah, Midnapore districts)  
**Category:** Society & Economics / Humanitarian Crisis  
**Key Figures:** Sheikh Hasina (Bangladesh Prime Minister, in office since January 2009), Mamata Banerjee (West Bengal opposition leader), Md. Sirajul Islam (Bangladesh Director General of Disaster Management), UN OCHA Regional Director for South Asia

## Summary

Tropical Storm Aila formed in the Bay of Bengal on May 23, 2009, and made landfall near the Sundarbans mangrove delta on the morning of May 25. With sustained winds of 110 km/h (70 mph) and a storm surge of 3–5 meters (reports from harder-hit char islands recorded surges as high as 10 meters locally), Aila inundated vast stretches of the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta — the world's largest and most densely populated river delta, home to approximately 35 million people across its low-lying coastal zones.

Official death tolls reached 190 in Bangladesh and 149 in India (West Bengal), totaling at least 339 confirmed fatalities; independent estimates placed the figure higher, with many deaths unrecorded in remote chars (river islands). More than 1 million people were displaced from their homes in the storm's immediate aftermath, and a further 2 million were left without adequate drinking water within 72 hours of landfall as saltwater contamination spread through freshwater aquifers and surface channels.

## Meteorological Profile

### Formation and Track

Aila originated as a depression in the north-central Bay of Bengal on May 23, 2009, strengthening rapidly due to unusually warm sea-surface temperatures (29–30°C, approximately 1°C above the 1981–2010 seasonal average). The depression intensified to tropical storm status by 06:00 UTC on May 24 and reached its peak intensity — 110 km/h sustained winds, central pressure approximately 976 hPa — within 12 hours of landfall.

The storm tracked north-northeast at 15–18 km/h, making landfall near Khulna, Bangladesh, at approximately 11:00 local time on May 25. Aila maintained tropical-storm intensity for roughly 6 hours after landfall before weakening over land; however, its slow forward speed (slowing to 10 km/h inland) allowed sustained rainfall of 100–150 mm to fall over West Bengal and southern Bangladesh through May 26, compounding surge flooding with riverine flooding as rivers overflowed their banks.

### Sundarbans Impact

The Sundarbans — a 10,000 km² mangrove forest straddling the India-Bangladesh border and designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997 — bore the brunt of Aila's initial landfall. The mangrove forest, which provides natural storm-surge attenuation, suffered significant canopy damage: Indian Forest Service surveys estimated 30% of tree cover in the affected zone was blown down or stripped. Saltwater intrusion into the mangrove soil matrix was documented at 15–25 km from the coast in the weeks following the storm.

The Sundarbans is also critical habitat for the Bengal tiger (approximately 400–500 individuals across the Indian-Bangladesh reserve). Wildlife surveys conducted in August–September 2009 found no evidence of catastrophic tiger mortality, though several tigers were reported in human-settled areas in the weeks after Aila — likely displacement due to habitat flooding.

## Humanitarian Crisis Phases

### Immediate Response (May 25–June 2009)

Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme (CPP) — established after Cyclone Bhola (1970) killed an estimated 300,000–500,000 people — had succeeded in pre-evacuating approximately 600,000 people from the highest-risk coastal areas before Aila's landfall. This preparedness significantly reduced mortality compared to earlier Bay of Bengal cyclones of similar intensity.

However, the 2009 early-warning infrastructure had significant gaps in the Sundarbans char areas, where small-island communities lacked radio access and transportation. In these zones, mortality rates were disproportionately high (estimated 10–15 per 1,000 population vs. 0.5–1 per 1,000 in evacuation-accessible areas).

UN OCHA issued an emergency flash appeal for $18.5 million within 48 hours of Aila's landfall, covering Bangladesh and West Bengal operations. Key immediate relief operations:
- Bangladesh Red Crescent Society deployed 55,000 volunteers in Khulna and Satkhira districts within 72 hours.
- World Food Programme (WFP) airlifted 500 metric tonnes of food supplements (high-energy biscuits, vegetable oil) to Khulna by May 28.
- UNICEF established 120 emergency water purification points in West Bengal's 24 Parganas district by June 5.

### Saltwater Contamination and Agricultural Collapse

Unlike high-wind cyclones that destroy structures rapidly but allow relatively swift rebuilding, Aila's storm surge drove saline water 20–40 km inland across agricultural fields, fish ponds, and freshwater aquifers. The consequences were prolonged and economically catastrophic:

**Agricultural land:** An estimated 200,000 hectares of rice paddy land in Bangladesh and 50,000 hectares in West Bengal were rendered infertile by saltwater intrusion. Recovery to pre-Aila productivity required 2–5 years of salt leaching through natural rainfall — in some areas, land was converted permanently from rice cultivation to brackish-water shrimp farming, fundamentally altering local livelihoods.

**Drinking water:** The delta's shallow tube-well infrastructure — the primary drinking water source for rural populations — was compromised when saltwater infiltrated the shallow aquifer layer (typically 10–30 meters depth). In Khulna district, water-quality surveys in July 2009 found that 78% of sampled tube wells exceeded WHO salinity thresholds; in 24 Parganas (India), the figure was 62%. Restoring potable water took 6–18 months in the most affected areas.

**Fisheries:** Bangladesh's artisanal fishing industry — employing an estimated 1.2 million people in the delta zone — was devastated: fish trap infrastructure, boats, and ponds were destroyed or contaminated. The BRAC economic survey of November 2009 estimated total fisheries losses at ₹3.5 billion (~$72 million) in Bangladesh alone.

### Medium-Term Displacement (June–December 2009)

Unlike typical disaster displacement — where affected populations return home within weeks — many Aila-affected families could not return to their land for months or years because of saltwater contamination. The Bangladesh government's disaster management reports (DDMC, September 2009) documented 250,000 people still displaced three months after the storm, with approximately 80,000 living in government-established relief camps.

This extended displacement drove significant rural-to-urban migration: BRAC and UNDP surveys conducted in late 2009 tracked out-migration from Aila-affected Khulna and Satkhira districts to Dhaka, Chittagong, and Khulna city at rates 40–60% higher than pre-Aila baselines. Young men disproportionately migrated to seek construction and industrial employment, creating female-headed household concentrations in remaining rural communities.

## Climate Change and Bangladesh's Frontline Status

### Contextualizing Aila

Aila struck Bangladesh eight months after Cyclone Sidr (November 2007), which killed approximately 3,400 people and caused $1.7 billion in damages — one of Bangladesh's worst disasters since Cyclone Bhola in 1970. The back-to-back major cyclone seasons of 2007 and 2009 reinforced the scientific and policy consensus that the Bay of Bengal was experiencing more-frequent intense cyclones, consistent with climate model projections of enhanced tropical cyclone intensity under warmer sea-surface temperatures.

Bangladesh's statistical position was stark: as of 2009, the country contributed approximately 0.3–0.4% of global cumulative greenhouse gas emissions while ranking among the world's top five most climate-vulnerable nations (Global Climate Risk Index 2010, Germanwatch). This disparity between emission responsibility and climate exposure became a central argument for "loss and damage" finance at [COP15 Copenhagen (December 2009)](/news/history-politics/cop15-copenhagen-2009/), where Bangladesh's climate negotiating team — supported by the Climate Vulnerable Forum (launched 2009) — advocated for binding adaptation finance from high-emission nations.

### Sea-Level Rise Vulnerability

The Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta — where Bangladesh sits — is subsiding naturally at 2–5 mm per year due to sediment compaction and tectonic processes, compounding observed sea-level rise of approximately 3–4 mm per year in the Bay of Bengal (consistent with global mean sea-level rise but accelerated by local factors). Aila's storm surge penetrated zones previously considered safe from 1-in-50-year storm events, suggesting that effective flood-risk envelopes were expanding faster than infrastructure could adapt.

The Bangladesh Climate Change Strategy and Action Plan (BCCSAP), finalized in September 2009 (just months after Aila), allocated 44% of its $5 billion 10-year budget to disaster risk reduction — the largest share of any climate adaptation plan globally at the time — explicitly citing Aila's lessons about surge-penetration depth and aquifer vulnerability.

## Policy and Reconstruction Outcomes

**Embankment rehabilitation:** Bangladesh's Department of Water Development (BWDB) identified 1,200 km of coastal embankments requiring emergency repair after Aila. Embankment failures — breaches in earthen flood defenses — were the principal mechanism of surge inland penetration. However, as of December 2009, only 380 km had been restored due to funding gaps, leaving communities exposed through the subsequent pre-monsoon cyclone season of 2010.

**Climate adaptation funding:** The World Bank's Bangladesh Climate Resilience Fund received $110 million in new pledges (from the UK DFID, Netherlands, Denmark, and the EU) in the six months following Aila, partly in response to the storm's visibility in international media. These funds supported mangrove restoration, embankment reinforcement, and freshwater infrastructure in the Sundarbans border zone.

**Shrimp farming transition:** In some permanently salt-affected areas, the Bangladesh government and NGOs (BRAC, World Fish Center) promoted managed shrimp aquaculture as a climate-adapted livelihood. By 2011, approximately 40,000 hectares that had been rice paddy before Aila were operating as brackish-water shrimp farms. This transition increased household incomes for some farmers but reduced food-crop production and reshaped community social structures.

**Comparison with [Cyclone Nargis humanitarian aftermath](/news/society-economics/cyclone-nargis-humanitarian-aftermath-2009/):** Aila struck less than one year after Myanmar's catastrophic Cyclone Nargis (May 2008), which killed 138,000 people and created a 1.5-million-person displacement crisis that extended through 2009. The juxtaposition of Nargis and Aila highlighted South Asia's extreme cyclone exposure in 2008–09 and drove both the UN's "One UN" disaster-response coordination reforms and the 2010 redesign of the Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF) allocation procedures.

## Significance

**Saltwater intrusion as a climate threat paradigm:** Aila was one of the first Bay of Bengal cyclones documented to cause multi-year agricultural disruption through saltwater contamination at scale. Climate scientists and agricultural economists studying Aila's aftermath contributed to a growing body of research on food-security vulnerability in river-delta systems, later reflected in the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (2014).

**Early-warning system advances:** Aila's differential mortality — far lower in CPP-covered areas than in remote chars — provided comparative evidence that early-warning and evacuation systems could dramatically reduce cyclone mortality even in high-exposure areas. This evidence influenced WHO and UNDRR recommendations on cyclone preparedness infrastructure investment globally.

**Displacement-climate nexus:** The extended rural-to-urban displacement following Aila provided one of the early empirical cases linking climate events to internal migration in densely populated South Asia. Studies by BRAC (2010), the Asian Development Bank (2011), and the International Organization for Migration (IOM, 2012) on Aila-driven displacement informed the Cancun Adaptation Framework's (COP16, 2010) work-program on migration, displacement, and relocation.

**Sundarbans conservation pressure:** Aila's damage to Sundarbans mangroves raised the profile of mangrove conservation as a cost-effective coastal protection strategy. The measurable capacity of healthy mangrove belts to attenuate storm surge — reducing surge height and inland penetration — became a widely cited argument for ecosystem-based adaptation in the delta.
