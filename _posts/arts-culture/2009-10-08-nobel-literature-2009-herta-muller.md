## Summary

On October 8, 2009, the Swedish Academy named Herta Müller the winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature. The citation praised the author "who, with the concentration of poetry and the frankness of prose, depicts the landscape of the dispossessed." The announcement in Stockholm was made by Peter Englund, who had taken office as the Academy's Permanent Secretary earlier that year.

Born in 1953 in Nițchidorf, a German-speaking village in the Banat region of Romania, Müller belonged to the country's Banat Swabian minority. Her early writing was censored by the Ceaușescu regime, and after she refused to cooperate with the Securitate secret police she faced harassment and dismissal from her job as a translator. She emigrated to West Germany in 1987. Much of her work — including the novels *Herztier* (published in English as *The Land of Green Plums*) and *Atemschaukel* (*The Hunger Angel*), the latter published in 2009 — draws on the experience of oppression, surveillance, and exile under communist dictatorship.

The award, worth 10 million Swedish kronor that year, was presented at the traditional Nobel ceremony in Stockholm on December 10, 2009, the anniversary of Alfred Nobel's death. Müller was the twelfth woman to receive the literature prize and the first German-language laureate since Elfriede Jelinek in 2004.

## Significance

The 2009 award brought international attention to a writer whose subject — the psychological toll of totalitarianism on the individual — resonated two decades after the fall of the Berlin Wall, an anniversary marked across Europe that same autumn. By honouring an author writing from the perspective of a persecuted ethnic minority within a former Eastern Bloc state, the Swedish Academy underscored the enduring literary reckoning with 20th-century dictatorship. The prize also substantially expanded the international readership and translation of Müller's previously little-known work outside the German-speaking world.
