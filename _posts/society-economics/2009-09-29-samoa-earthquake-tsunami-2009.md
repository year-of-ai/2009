## Summary

At about 6:48 a.m. local time on September 29, 2009, a magnitude-8.1 earthquake struck the seafloor midway between Samoa and American Samoa, near the northern end of the Tonga Trench — one of the most seismically active regions on Earth. The event was an unusual "doublet," involving closely spaced ruptures on both a normal fault in the outer rise and the adjacent subduction thrust within a few minutes of one another. The seabed displacement generated a tsunami that reached the surrounding islands within minutes, leaving little time for evacuation.

Tsunami waves — locally reaching heights of roughly 14 metres and running far inland in low-lying areas — devastated coastal villages in the independent state of Samoa, the U.S. territory of American Samoa, and the northern Tongan island of Niuatoputapu. At least 192 people were killed: about 149 in Samoa, 34 in American Samoa, and 9 in Tonga. Thousands were displaced, and the United Nations reported that more than 3,000 Samoans were left homeless. The Pacific Tsunami Warning Center issued a warning shortly after the quake, and the disaster prompted a major international humanitarian and reconstruction response, including aid from the United States, Australia, and New Zealand.

## Significance

The 2009 Samoa tsunami was one of the deadliest natural disasters of the year and the worst in modern Samoan history. It exposed the difficulty of warning near-source coastal populations, where destructive waves can arrive within 10–20 minutes, and spurred investment in community education, evacuation routes, and warning systems across the South Pacific. Post-disaster needs assessments led by the affected governments and international partners guided reconstruction and informed later regional disaster-preparedness planning. The event stands alongside the year's other major seismic disasters, including the [L'Aquila earthquake]({{ '/news/society-economics/laquila-earthquake-2009/' | relative_url }}) in Italy.
