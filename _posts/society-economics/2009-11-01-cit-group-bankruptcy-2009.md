## Summary

CIT Group, Inc. — founded in 1908 and one of the largest commercial-finance companies in the United States — filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York on November 1, 2009. At the time of filing, CIT had approximately $60–65 billion in assets on its balance sheet (not the $2.3 trillion figure for managed/originated assets), making it one of the five largest corporate bankruptcies in U.S. history by book assets.

CIT had for more than a century specialized in equipment-based lending, factoring, trade finance, and working-capital facilities for mid-sized and small businesses — ranging from retail chains that used CIT as their main receivables factor to small manufacturers leasing CIT-financed machinery. Its customer base included approximately one million small and mid-sized businesses at the time of its collapse.

## Road to bankruptcy

**2008 crisis exposure**: CIT had diversified into subprime student lending, home lending, and leveraged buyout financing during the mid-2000s credit boom. When those markets collapsed in 2008, CIT's loan book deteriorated sharply. The company became a bank holding company in December 2008 to gain access to Federal Reserve credit facilities, and received a $2.33 billion capital injection from TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program) in December 2008.

**Failed rescue attempts (July 2009)**: By mid-2009, CIT's liquidity position had become critical. The company sought an additional federal backstop but was denied: the FDIC declined to guarantee CIT's debt issuances under the Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program (TLGP) on July 15–16, 2009, citing CIT's weakened financial condition and concerns about moral hazard. The Treasury Department also declined further TARP assistance. Attempts by creditors (led by Carl Icahn) to arrange a $3 billion private emergency credit facility provided only temporary relief.

**Chapter 11 filing**: With no government backstop and mounting debt maturities, CIT filed for Chapter 11 on November 1, 2009, with a pre-negotiated (prepackaged) reorganization plan already approved by most creditor classes. The process was extraordinarily fast: the bankruptcy court confirmed the plan on December 8, 2009, and CIT emerged from bankruptcy on December 10, 2009 — approximately 39 days after filing — one of the fastest reorganizations of a company of its size in U.S. history.

## Reorganization terms

Under the prepackaged plan:
- Senior bondholders received approximately **\$0.70 on the dollar** through a debt-for-equity conversion
- TARP preferred stock holders (the U.S. government) received nothing — the $2.33 billion TARP investment was a total loss to taxpayers
- The reorganized CIT emerged with a substantially deleveraged balance sheet, approximately $10.5 billion in new long-term notes, and a new equity base distributed to former creditors
- John Thain (former CEO of Merrill Lynch) was named CEO in February 2010 to lead the restructured company

## Significance

**Scale and systemic impact**: CIT's bankruptcy was the fifth-largest corporate bankruptcy in U.S. history at the time (by book assets), surpassed only by Lehman Brothers ($691 billion, September 2008), Washington Mutual ($327 billion, September 2008), WorldCom ($103 billion, 2002), and General Motors ($91 billion, June 2009). Unlike those failures, CIT's collapse uniquely threatened small business credit.

**Small-business credit channel**: CIT was a critical intermediary for small businesses, particularly in retail and manufacturing. Its factoring operations alone supported approximately 2,000 retailers and their suppliers. The disruption to CIT's operations during bankruptcy caused weeks of uncertainty over receivables payments and credit facilities — a direct drag on small-business investment and hiring at the depth of the recession.

**TARP loss**: The $2.33 billion TARP loss on CIT was the largest single TARP investment loss up to that point and became a focus of Congressional oversight about the efficacy of government capital injections into troubled financial institutions.

**Policy lesson**: The episode illustrated the limits of the TARP bank-stabilization model — capital alone could not address structural solvency problems at institutions with fundamentally impaired loan books, and highlighted gaps in the federal government's toolkit for resolving large non-bank financial companies. These lessons directly influenced the Dodd-Frank Act's Orderly Liquidation Authority (Title II), enacted July 2010, which created a resolution mechanism for systemically important non-bank financial companies.

## Aftermath

CIT Group emerged from bankruptcy in December 2009 as a leaner company and continued to operate in commercial lending. In 2015, CIT acquired OneWest Bank (the successor to IndyMac Federal Bank), expanding into retail banking. CIT was ultimately acquired by First Citizens BancShares in January 2022 for approximately $2.2 billion — completing a 13-year cycle from near-collapse to acquisition.
