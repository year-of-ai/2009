## Summary

Space Shuttle Atlantis launched from Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39A on May 11, 2009, carrying a crew of seven on the fifth and final servicing mission (SM4 / STS-125) to the Hubble Space Telescope. Over 12 days, 21 hours, and 37 minutes in orbit, the crew conducted five extravehicular activities (EVAs) totaling approximately 37 hours of spacewalk time—among the most complex servicing operations in NASA history.

The mission's principal instrument installations were:

- **Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3)**: replaced the Wide Field and Planetary Camera 2 (WFPC2). WFC3 can image simultaneously across ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared wavelengths, providing a factor-of-15 to 35 improvement in survey efficiency compared to its predecessor.
- **Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS)**: a UV spectrograph optimized for detecting faint point sources and measuring the large-scale structure of the universe through absorption spectroscopy of background quasars.

Beyond new instruments, the crew repaired two existing instruments that had been offline:

- **Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS)**: failed in January 2007 due to a short circuit. Astronauts performed a first-ever in-orbit repair of a circuit board, restoring ACS's Wide Field Channel.
- **Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph (STIS)**: failed in August 2004 due to a power supply failure. The team replaced all four power-supply boards, restoring STIS to full functionality.

Hardware replacements included six rate sensor units (gyroscopes), six nickel-hydrogen battery modules, a Science Instrument Command and Data Handling (SI C&DH) unit, and new thermal-blanket panels on three Equipment Bays. Atlantis undocked from Hubble on May 19, 2009, and landed at Edwards Air Force Base on May 24, 2009.

## Significance

STS-125 was the last human visit to Hubble; NASA's management decision in 2004 to cancel earlier servicing plans was reversed after public and scientific pressure, making this mission especially consequential. The instruments installed have since enabled transformative discoveries:

- WFC3 produced the **eXtreme Deep Field (XDF)** in 2012, imaging galaxies as far as 13.2 billion light-years away.
- COS revealed that the warm intergalactic medium surrounding galaxies is far denser than previously modeled, reframing understanding of galactic feedback and baryonic matter distribution.
- Restored STIS provided high-precision ultraviolet spectroscopy underpinning measurements of dark-matter halos and supermassive black hole masses.

The new hardware extended Hubble's operational life well past its original 15-year design lifetime (launched April 24, 1990). As of the mid-2020s, Hubble remained scientifically active, complementing the James Webb Space Telescope launched in December 2021.

The mission demonstrated the enduring value of human spaceflight as an instrument-maintenance and repair capability, reinforcing arguments for crewed servicing of future space observatories.
